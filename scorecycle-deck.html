A shredded flake in a rising airflow has exactly one balance point.

light out
heavy out, air in below
drag
weight
Above the balance point it rises. Below it, it falls. That threshold is the entire separation.
Density alone does not decide.
It is density together with thickness. How much material sits behind every square millimetre facing the flow.
dense, thin light, thick
both sit on the same balance point
Two completely different materials can suspend at the same velocity. The column has nothing to separate them by.
Zhu, Zhang and Feng. Waste Management 131 (2021).

So we change the velocity while it is running.

1
Particles hang in the belly
Their balance point matches the air exactly, so they neither rise nor fall. Every one is a coin flip.
2
+0.0%
The camera watches, we nudge
Vision finds the group that is not moving. At the same moment we change the air speed a few percent and see which way they go. That is how the machine learns which way to move next.
3
We park it in a gap
The stuck group moves to a speed almost no flakes sit at. Far fewer coin flips.
You cannot delete the stuck group. You can park it where almost nothing sits.

This is the model running.

The airflow on screen is not scripted. It is the output of the loop.

Where the model goes next.

We choose the width
A wider middle slows the air down. Slower air gives more flakes time to sort themselves out. Today nobody picks this width. The machine just came that way.
We measure the flake
A lamp shines on every flake as it drops in. We read how thick it really is. Right now the machine has to guess, and a flat photo cannot show thickness.
We chain the columns
Put three columns in a row. Each one sorts the leftovers from the one before it. Three easy jobs are better than one hard job.
We move the loop over
Other sorting machines have the same problem. Watch, nudge, keep what worked. The same loop runs on all of them.
Every mechanical separator in the world is tuned once, then run blind for life.
